ArtPoW: A Free Monthly NFT Earned by Proof of Work

The ArtPoW project

Abstract. We describe a collection of non-fungible tokens on Polygon in which a new edition, created by a guest artist, opens every month and can be claimed for free by anyone, directly on-chain or through a website. A claim requires a proof of work tied to its recipient, so it cannot be stolen and anyone may submit it. The difficulty follows a linear schedule of one thousand tokens per month: every token minted ahead of the schedule makes the next one harder, while time eases it back to a floor of about one minute of browser mining. There is no cap on the number of tokens; supply regulates itself, and an attacker's excess grows only with the logarithm of its computing power. The seed of each month is an accumulator of every claim of the previous month, so the puzzle of a month is unknown until the previous month ends. Metadata are generated on-chain, the contract cannot be upgraded, its parameters are constants, and no royalty is charged.

1. Introduction

Free distributions of digital collectibles usually face two problems. Without a cost, automated accounts claim most of the supply; with a price or an allow-list, the distribution is no longer open. Proof of work, introduced as an anti-abuse measure by Hashcash [9] and used by Bitcoin to order transactions [1], gives a third option: a claim costs a measurable amount of computation, which anyone can provide and anyone can verify.

This paper describes ArtPoW, an ERC-721 contract [3] that applies this idea to a monthly edition. Each month, a guest artist creates the artwork; any address can claim one token by mining a nonce in a browser, from the command line or with any other software. The contract keeps the average issuance close to one thousand tokens per month without a hard cap, by adjusting the difficulty after every claim against a linear schedule. The rest of the design aims at staying simple: one seed mechanism, no transaction to open a month, no grace periods, metadata entirely on-chain, and administrator powers that never extend to the holders' tokens.

Proof of work regulates the supply, not who receives it. A single modern GPU computes as many hashes as about a thousand browsers; when farms compete, they obtain most of the tokens. Section 9 quantifies this and the other limits of the design.

2. Monthly Editions

Months are scheduled by administrators, in order and without gaps in their numbering. Each month has a start, an end and a content: a label (for example “November 2026”), the artist's name, an optional address receiving the artist proof, the URI of a square artwork and an optional animation URI. The contract enforces that a month:

  • lasts between 25 and 35 days, which covers calendar months and rejects typing errors that would become final once the month starts;
  • starts in the future and not before the end of the previous month, so months never overlap;
  • has a non-empty label and image;
  • can be edited until it starts, and is then locked, dates and content included.

A month ends at its scheduled time whatever happens. Claims are accepted while start≤now<end\mathit{start} \le \mathit{now} < \mathit{end}; if the next month is not scheduled yet, no claim is possible until it starts. Months are expected to be contiguous: the next one starts when the previous one ends.

3. Proof of Work

To claim a token of month mm for a recipient rr, one finds a nonce nn such that

H=keccak256(abi.encode(seedm, m, r, n))<T,H = \mathrm{keccak256}\big(\mathrm{abi.encode}(\mathit{seed}_m,\ m,\ r,\ n)\big) < T ,

where TT is the target at the time the claim is included in a block (Section 5). The encoded message is 128 bytes long, a single Keccak-256 block [8], so every attempt costs exactly one permutation, on a GPU as in a browser.

The recipient is part of the hash. A proof found for rr is useless for any other address: whoever submits it, the token goes to rr. Proofs therefore cannot be stolen, and anyone may submit a claim on behalf of anyone else. Each address can claim at most one mined token per month; transferring the token does not allow a second claim.

A proof is checked against the target at inclusion, not at the time it was found. Time only makes the target easier; claims included in the meantime make it harder, by about 7% each. Miners therefore aim at the target the contract would have after one or two more claims, which costs about 15% more work and makes stale proofs negligible in practice.

4. Seed Chain

The seed of month 1 is the hash of the block preceding the deployment. Every month keeps an accumulator, initialised to keccak256(seedm,m)\mathrm{keccak256}(\mathit{seed}_m, m) and updated by every claim:

accm←keccak256(accm, blockhash(b−1), r, n),\mathit{acc}_m \leftarrow \mathrm{keccak256}\big(\mathit{acc}_m,\ \mathrm{blockhash}(b-1),\ r,\ n\big),

where bb is the block of the claim. The seed of the next month is the final accumulator: seedm+1=accm\mathit{seed}_{m+1} = \mathit{acc}_m. Since every claim changes the accumulator, the puzzle of month m+1m+1 is unknown until month mm ends. A month without any claim keeps its initial accumulator, which is accepted: a month nobody claims is a month nobody is trying to pre-mine.

Artist and team proofs (Section 6) do not touch the accumulator, so administrators cannot influence the next seed by choosing when to mint them.

5. Difficulty Schedule

Let kk be the number of tokens already mined in the month and S(t)S(t) the number the schedule expects at time tt:

S(t)=1000⋅t−startend−start.S(t) = 1000 \cdot \frac{t - \mathit{start}}{\mathit{end} - \mathit{start}} .

The target is

T(k,t)=min⁡ ⁣(Tmax⁡, Tmax⁡⋅2 (S(t)−k)/10),Tmax⁡=2256−28.T(k, t) = \min\!\Big(T_{\max},\ T_{\max} \cdot 2^{\,(S(t) - k)/10}\Big), \qquad T_{\max} = 2^{256-28}.

At the floor Tmax⁡T_{\max}, a valid hash takes 228≈2.7×1082^{28} \approx 2.7 \times 10^{8} attempts on average, about one minute in a browser. Each token minted ahead of the schedule multiplies the difficulty by 21/10≈1.072^{1/10} \approx 1.07; ten tokens ahead double it. Time works the other way: with no claim, the difficulty halves every ten scheduled tokens, about 7.2 hours in a 30-day month. Behind the schedule, the difficulty stays at the floor and the delay can be caught up within the month: if few people claimed, whoever arrives can take the rest. Every month restarts from the floor and targets one thousand tokens whatever its length.

The rule is the per-month form of the ASERT algorithm used by Bitcoin Cash [2], with the claims of the month in place of blocks. The target changes after every claim: a burst raises the difficulty as it happens, and no window lets a fast miner claim many tokens at a stale target.

The contract computes x⋅2a/bx \cdot 2^{a/b} in fixed point with the cubic approximation of aserti3-2d [2]: 16-bit fractional exponent, maximum relative error 0.0117%, monotonic. The implementation is checked bit for bit against an independent Python port on random vectors, with the production floor.

Calculations

A miner with hash rate RR that claims continuously reaches the equilibrium where it finds one token per scheduled interval I=(end−start)/1000I = (\mathit{end} - \mathit{start})/1000, that is RI=228⋅2n/10R I = 2^{28} \cdot 2^{n/10}. Its lead on the schedule is therefore

n=10log⁡2R I228.n = 10 \log_2 \frac{R\, I}{2^{28}} .

For one GPU at 5×1095 \times 10^9 hashes per second and a 30-day month (I=2592I = 2592 s), n≈156n \approx 156; ten GPUs add only 10log⁡210≈3310 \log_2 10 \approx 33 tokens. A farm can take most of a month's tokens, but the month's total stays close to one thousand plus this lead.

A miner can also accumulate work WW before a month starts, whenever the seed is known in advance (Section 9), and submit the results at once. The jj-th token at the start of the month needs a hash below Tmax⁡2−j/10T_{\max} 2^{-j/10}, and WW attempts yield on average W2−28−j/10W 2^{-28-j/10} such hashes, so the burst KK satisfies W2−28−K/10≈KW 2^{-28-K/10} \approx K:

K≈10(log⁡2W−28−log⁡2K).K \approx 10 \left(\log_2 W - 28 - \log_2 K\right).

6. Artist and Team Proofs

Besides mined tokens, each month may have one artist proof, sent to the artist's address, and one team proof, sent to an address set by the administrators. Proofs are minted without work by claimReserved, which anyone may call during the month; the token always goes to the designated address, so the website or an administrator can mint them as soon as the month starts. Each proof exists at most once per month.

Proofs stay out of the numbering, the total and the difficulty schedule, and do not touch the seed. They are also independent of mined claims: the artist and the team may mine a regular token as well. Since anyone can mine a claim on behalf of any address, this keeps a third party from blocking a proof by mining a regular token for the artist first.

The collection charges no royalty. Royalties are optional on most marketplaces and would amount to a fee on the work of artists who do not receive it; the proofs give the artist and the team a share that is valuable only if the collection is.

7. Metadata

Token metadata are a JSON document generated by the contract and returned by tokenURI, with no server. A mined token is named after its month and number, for example “November 2026 ∙\bullet #256”, and shows the final total once the month is over: “November 2026 ∙\bullet #256 / 1035”. Its attributes are the month, the artist, the edition (Mined, Artist proof or Team proof), the number, the total, the difficulty at which it was mined and the number of leading zero bits of its hash, a rarity attribute that the miner can improve by searching longer. Proofs have no number, difficulty or zero bits.

The total is read when the metadata are requested, so closing a month requires no transaction. Since marketplaces cache metadata, anyone can call announceMonthTotal once after the month ends; it emits the EIP-4906 batch update event [4] over the month's tokens, which are contiguous because months never overlap.

Collection metadata follow ERC-7572 [5]: contractURI returns the name, the description, the logo and the website, the last two set by administrators. The website also gives every token a link to its own page. The owner function of ERC-173 [6], which marketplaces use to decide who manages the collection page, returns an address set by the administrators; it grants no right in the contract.

Images are referenced by URI. The recommended practice is an ipfs:// content identifier pinned by two independent services, with a permanent copy on Arweave from which the same identifier can be pinned again. Very small images can be stored on-chain as data: URIs, at about 980 gas per byte.

8. Administration

The contract has no proxy and cannot be upgraded; its parameters are constants. Administrators form a list, built on OpenZeppelin's access control [7], in which any administrator can add or remove others, so that losing one wallet does not lose the contract. Adding an administrator takes two steps: an administrator calls proposeAdmin, then the proposed address calls acceptAdmin itself. Only an address able to send a transaction on this network can become an administrator, which rules out the zero address, typing errors and multisig wallets deployed on another network. The last administrator cannot be removed.

Administrators schedule months that have not started, set the team address, the owner address, the logo and the website, and can recover assets sent to the contract by mistake: native tokens sent by forced transfer, ERC-20 tokens and NFTs of other collections. They can never move, burn or freeze holders' tokens, and tokens of this collection sent to the contract stay there for good.

In case of a flaw, any administrator can call shutdown. It is irreversible and immediate: claims, proofs and scheduling stop for good, the running month is considered closed, and transfers, metadata and recovery keep working. A single administrator can trigger it on purpose: stopping a contract by mistake or after a theft of keys is better than leaving one running with a known flaw, or one that a rogue administrator could keep from being stopped.

9. Security Considerations

Farms. Proof of work cannot tell a person from a machine. With one GPU equivalent to about a thousand browsers, a farm keeps the difficulty at a level where a browser would need weeks per token. The design bounds the supply, not the farms' share; only a proof of personhood could, which is outside the scope of a contract that anyone can use directly.

Pre-mining. The seed of a month is public before its start in three cases: between the deployment and the first month, during a gap between two months, and speculatively at the end of a quiet month, when the last claim may be final. Work accumulated in these windows can be submitted as a burst when the month opens. The schedule bounds it as computed above:

Pre-mined workBurst at the start of the month
1 GPU for 3 hours≈108\approx 108 tokens
1 GPU for 1 week≈160\approx 160 tokens
100 GPUs for 1 month≈242\approx 242 tokens

This risk is accepted: the first month opens before the project is widely known, and later months are contiguous. The participant who submits the very last claim of a month, or the validator producing that block, learns the next seed seconds before others; the schedule throttles any resulting burst in the same way.

Front-running. A pending claim reveals its nonce, but a copy still mints to the intended recipient. A competitor can only make a pending proof insufficient by mining claims of its own, which is mining.

Compromised administrator. A compromised administrator cannot touch holders' tokens or mint more than two free tokens per month. It could remove the other administrators, schedule a month with malicious content, which becomes permanent once the month starts, point the website link of every token to another site, take over the collection page through owner, or stop the contract. Administration is therefore meant to be held by a multisig wallet, with alerts on the administrative events.

Network. Block timestamps can be shifted by a few seconds, which moves the schedule by a negligible fraction of a token. Blocks can be reorganised shortly after they are produced; miners should wait for finality before mining on a new month's seed, and interfaces before displaying token numbers.

Implementation. All state changes of a claim happen before the only external call, the ERC-721 receiver hook, and re-entering claims leave the state consistent. The contract makes no arbitrary call, has no delegatecall or self-destruct, and embeds administrator-provided strings in its JSON only after rejecting quotes, backslashes and control characters. Unit, fuzz, invariant and differential tests cover the rules described here, and the compiler version is pinned so that the deployed bytecode can be verified.

10. Measurements and Simulations

The reference miner is a Keccak-f permutation written in WebAssembly. It reaches 2 million hashes per second per thread in the V8 engine on a 2016 desktop processor, 7.5 million on four cores, twenty times faster than a pure JavaScript implementation. Assuming a median browser at 4 million hashes per second, the floor takes 67 seconds on average and 46 seconds in median. A GPU computes about 5×1095 \times 10^9 single-block Keccak-256 hashes per second.

A Monte-Carlo simulation of a 30-day month models people arriving over the month, each mining in a browser with a log-normal hash rate and giving up after a log-normal patience, and farms mining continuously with unlimited addresses. Results, averaged over five runs:

When demand is low, everyone is served within minutes. Above one thousand claims a month, the difficulty limits issuance and patient people get the tokens. Whatever the attackers' power, a month stays between one thousand and twelve hundred tokens, while farms take most of them.

12. Conclusion

We have described a free monthly NFT in which access is open to anyone able to compute, and issuance regulates itself without a cap. Proofs of work are bound to their recipients, so they cannot be stolen and can be submitted by anyone; a chain of accumulators keeps each month's puzzle unknown until the previous month ends; a difficulty that follows a linear schedule after every claim keeps a month close to one thousand tokens, an attacker's lead growing only with the logarithm of its power. Metadata live on-chain, administrators cannot touch the holders' tokens, and the contract can be stopped but never changed. The design does not decide who wins the tokens when machines compete with people; it only makes the cost of each token explicit and the rules the same for everyone.
